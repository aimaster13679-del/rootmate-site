How to find a life partner when you live abroad

Moving abroad changes how you meet people. Friends from school are back home, colleagues are busy, and family introductions arrive from thousands of kilometres away. Here is a practical way to look for a life partner for marriage when you have built your life in a new country.

1. Decide what "home" means for you

Before you look, be honest about where you want to live in five years. Staying in Canada? Moving back one day? Open to another country? This one answer rules out more mismatches than anything else, and it is the first thing worth asking a match.

2. Look where people like you already are

Large matrimony sites mostly show people in your home country. Community groups, temples, gurdwaras, mosques, churches and cultural associations are good, but small. A matrimony app made for people already living abroad, such as NeuMarry, shows you verified members in your own city first.

3. Talk about values early

Shared roots help, but they are not enough. Talk early about family roles, careers after marriage, children, faith, money and where parents will live later. NeuMarry asks nine short questions about these topics and shows where you and a match agree before you say hello.

4. Meet in person sooner

Weeks of chatting can build a picture that does not survive the first coffee. If you live in the same city, meet in a public place within a couple of weeks. Hosted events like a Family Mixer make the first meeting easier, with families in the same room.

5. Involve family the right way

Parents often want to help. Let them suggest profiles, meet other families and share their experience, while the conversations stay between the two of you. See NeuMarry for parents.

6. Stay safe

Only talk to verified people, never send money to someone you have not met, and be careful with anyone who avoids meeting or video calls. Read our safety guide.

7. Give it time, but not forever

Set a simple rhythm: a few new profiles a week, one or two conversations at a time, and a decision after you have met in person and families have spoken. Clear and kind answers, yes or no, make the process better for everyone.
